## The role, in one line

**Energy is the input that decides how much computing gets built**. The corridor's energy story is made of light: solar today, laser fusion on the horizon, and the power systems that keep compute running.

Photonic chips cut what a data center needs. Energy supplies what it still needs. This document covers the supply side: light as energy, and energy for compute.

## WHY energy

Compute is now limited by electricity more than by anything else. In Colorado the numbers are concrete. Xcel Energy's 2024 resource plan projects a 19 percent rise in peak demand, to 8.6 gigawatts, by 2031. The same filings estimate large-customer demand, mostly data centers, at 3 to 20 terawatt-hours a year. That is growth 10 to 25 times faster than historical averages, per Xcel filings reported by the Colorado Sun (2025-2026).

A builder siting compute needs two things from a region: power that exists and power that stays cheap and clean. The corridor's answer is to build both the compute that needs less (the photonics story) and the energy that powers the rest (this story). Solar is light as energy today. Laser fusion is light as energy on the horizon, and the corridor's fusion work is laser-driven, which keeps it on-thesis.

## HOW it works, in plain language

The near-term answer is proven and gets cheaper every year: solar, wind, and storage. Colorado has abundant sun, and the eastern plains carry some of the best wind in the interior West. The missing piece is storage, holding a summer afternoon of sunlight for a winter night. New transmission is wiring that generation to the Front Range, where the compute sits.

The horizon answer is fusion, the same reaction that powers the sun. Laser (inertial) fusion fires enormous laser pulses at a small fuel target, compressing it until atoms fuse and release energy. It is hard because it means creating matter hotter than the core of the sun, on demand, many times a second. The honest status: no fusion plant sells power yet, anywhere. But the work has crossed from physics question to engineering race, and the corridor is in the laser-driven lane of that race.

## What it unlocks

- **Compute hosted on clean power.** New solar, wind, and storage built to meet compute demand leaves the grid with more clean capacity than it started with, not less.
- **Always-on carbon-free power for facilities that never sleep.** Data centers run flat out around the clock. Storage, geothermal, and eventually fusion are the always-on pieces that let clean power match that profile.
- **A corridor that helps invent the next power source.** Laser fusion is the light-based path to fusion energy. The machines for it are being built in Denver and up the road in Fort Collins.
- **Power that gets cheaper, not scarcer.** Clean generation has no fuel cost, so a grid built on it pushes the long-run price of power down.

## How fast (the honest timeline)

The renewable layer is now. Colorado's roadmap targets 100 percent clean electricity by 2040, a goal, not a present fact (Polis administration roadmap, Colorado Energy Office). Every coal plant in the state is scheduled to close by 2031, per the state's utility Clean Energy Plans. Renewables supplied 43 percent of the state's generated electricity as of 2024, per Colorado state climate reporting. Xcel's Power Pathway, a 550-mile transmission loop for eastern-plains wind and solar, is partly in service, per Xcel. Two segments energized in spring 2024. The rest are scheduled to complete in 2026 and 2027.

Always-on clean power for compute is moving from pilot to purchase order. Google has contracted 115 megawatts of geothermal from Fervo in Nevada and announced a 150-megawatt agreement with Ormat in February 2026. Meta announced contracts for about 300 megawatts in 2026 from XGS Energy and Sage Geosystems. Fervo reports its Cape Station fully contracted at 500 megawatts, with $421 million in project financing closed in March 2026.

Fusion is the horizon, and the milestones are dated. In Denver, Xcimer announced the start of operations for Phoenix in June 2026. The company calls Phoenix the largest privately owned laser system in the world and reports just over $111 million raised. Nationally, Commonwealth Fusion expects its SPARC machine substantially complete by the end of 2026, with first plasma targeted for 2027. Helion is building to deliver 50 megawatts to Microsoft by 2028 under the first fusion power purchase agreement, per company announcements. The honest line: no fusion plant sells power yet, and the first ones are years out, not quarters. The direction is set, and some of it is being invented here.

## WHY this corridor

- **The national energy lab is in Golden.** NLR (formerly NREL) was renamed the National Laboratory of the Rockies by the Department of Energy on 2025-12-01. It has run national solar and clean-energy research since 1977, when it opened as the Solar Energy Research Institute. Light as energy has been a Golden discipline for almost fifty years.
- **The laser-fusion company is in Denver.** Xcimer runs its laser program in a 74,000-square-foot Denver facility, per the company. Phoenix, its prototype system, started operations in June 2026. The published roadmap targets larger machines in 2028 and 2031; those dates are the company's goals.
- **The giant lasers are just up the road.** Colorado State University is building the ATLAS facility with Marvel Fusion on its Foothills Campus in Fort Collins. CSU reports a $160 million, 77,626-square-foot laser building, topped out in April 2026 and on track for substantial completion in December 2026. Fort Collins sits outside the corridor. The honest claim is corridor-adjacent, part of the same Front Range laser ecosystem.
- **The grid build is real and dated.** Power Pathway segments are energizing through 2027, per Xcel. The state's coal fleet is scheduled to close by 2031 under utility Clean Energy Plans, and the 2040 clean-electricity target frames the build.

The honest version of the claim: the corridor will not commercialize fusion alone, and Colorado's grid is not yet clean. But the national energy lab is in Golden, and a funded laser-fusion company operates in Denver. CSU's ATLAS laser facility finishes in Fort Collins in 2026. The transmission to bring plains wind and sun to the Front Range is energizing now. That is a place that can power the compute era and help invent the power source that comes after.

## Internal honesty guardrails

1. **Fusion is a future claim.** No fusion plant sells power yet, anywhere. "The corridor is in the laser-fusion race" is true. "Fusion powers the corridor," stated as done, is not. Keep fusion in the future tense.
2. **The grid is not clean yet.** The 100 percent clean-electricity target is a 2040 goal. Renewables were 43 percent of state generation as of 2024, per state reporting. Never state the goal as current fact.
3. **ATLAS is a research facility, not a power plant, and it is not in the corridor.** It is a laser research building in Fort Collins, completing in 2026 per CSU. Claim the Front Range laser ecosystem, not a corridor address and not electricity generation.
4. **The corridor's fusion lane is laser (inertial) fusion.** Magnetic-confinement fusion is led elsewhere (Commonwealth Fusion in Massachusetts, others). Do not claim magnetic fusion as an in-state strength.
5. **Company roadmaps are goals.** Xcimer's 2028 and 2031 machines, SPARC's 2027 first plasma, and Helion's 2028 delivery are company targets, not deliveries. Attribute them and keep them dated.
6. **Superlatives carry their owner.** "Largest privately owned laser system in the world" is Xcimer's description of Phoenix. Attribute it.
7. **NLR naming.** NLR is "formerly NREL" on first reference, renamed 2025-12-01. The rename also broadened the lab's stated mission beyond renewables; do not quote its old mission language as current.
8. **Statewide policy stays with Colorado AI.** Geothermal policy programs, data-center rate design, and energy legislation are Colorado AI's territory. This document claims corridor assets and dated facts, not policy positions.
